A patient arrives at a new hospital and their history does not arrive with them.
They repeat the same story, repeat the same tests, and the clinician makes a
decision with less than they should have. This is not an edge case across much
of the continent — it is the default.

The record problem is a **data problem**: fragmentation across institutions, no
shared standard for exchange, no reliable audit trail, and no meaningful
ownership by the patient. Those are the specific properties distributed ledgers
claim to address, which is why the idea deserves serious examination rather than
either enthusiasm or dismissal.

## The research

I am conducting a **systematic review of blockchain-based electronic health records
in African healthcare** — surveying what has actually been proposed, piloted and
evaluated on the continent, rather than what is theoretically possible.

The review is **ongoing**. Findings will be added here once the work is complete.

## The position I hold

Blockchain is not a health system. It does not create records that do not exist,
train the staff who enter them, or supply the connectivity a clinic does not
have. Any honest account has to start there.

What it can plausibly contribute is narrower and still worth having: a
tamper-evident audit trail, a consent model the patient actually controls, and a
way for institutions that do not trust each other to agree on a shared history
without one of them owning it. Whether that is worth the operational cost is an
empirical question — which is exactly what the review is for.

## Communicating it

Research that stays inside a PDF changes nothing. I make short explainer videos
working through these ideas for people who are not going to read a systematic
review — clinicians, students, and anyone curious about why their medical record
behaves the way it does.
