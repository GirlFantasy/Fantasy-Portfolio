## Context

Peer-to-peer sexual abuse within medical training environments is
under-researched, and the conditions that make it hard to study are the same
conditions that let it persist: close-knit cohorts, long shared training years,
steep informal hierarchies, and strong disincentives to report.

Before an institution can respond to a problem, it needs to know its shape. That
requires measuring it.

## The question

**What do medical students in Abakaliki know about peer-to-peer sexual abuse,
how do they perceive it, and what have they experienced?**

Separating knowledge, perception and experience matters. A population can be
well-informed and still normalise the behaviour, or recognise it clearly and
still have no viable route to report it. Those are different problems requiring
different responses.

## Method

A **quantitative descriptive cross-sectional study** conducted among medical
students in Abakaliki, Ebonyi State, Nigeria.

## Why it matters

Medical training shapes the profession that follows it. A cohort that learns
early which behaviours go unnamed and unaddressed carries that lesson into
practice, into supervision, and into the next cohort it trains.

Descriptive research is the necessary first step. You cannot design an
intervention for a problem whose prevalence and shape you have not established.

## A note on method

This project sits some distance from on-chain analysis, and that distance is
part of why it belongs here. Survey design, instrument construction, careful
handling of sensitive disclosures and honest descriptive statistics are the same
discipline applied to a very different subject — define the question precisely,
collect data that can actually answer it, and report what you found rather than
what you hoped for.
